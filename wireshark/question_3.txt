First, let's open up the webpage so Wireshark has data to capture.

### Step 1: Open the website

1. Open your web browser (like Chrome, Firefox, or Edge).
2. Copy and paste this exact link into your address bar and press Enter:
**[http://gaia.cs.umass.edu/wireshark-labs/HTTP-wireshark-file1.html]()**
3. Leave that browser window open.

---

### Step 2: Set up Wireshark to record

1. Open the **Wireshark** application on your computer.
2. You will see a list of connections on the main screen (like *Wi-Fi*, *Ethernet*, or *Local Area Connection*). Look for the one that has a jagged line moving next to it—that means it's active.
3. **Double-click** on that active connection (usually **Wi-Fi**).
4. Suddenly, the screen will fill up with hundreds of colorful lines. Wireshark is now recording your internet traffic!

---

### Step 3: Refresh the webpage to capture it

1. Go back to your web browser where you opened that UMass website.
2. **Refresh/Reload** the page (press `F5` or click the circular arrow icon).
3. Go back to **Wireshark**.
4. Look at the very top left corner of the Wireshark window and click the **Red Square button** to STOP the capture.

---

### Step 4: Find our webpage traffic

Right now, you are looking at thousands of confusing lines. Let's hide everything except our webpage.

1. At the very top of the Wireshark window, there is a long white bar that says **"Apply a display filter..."**.
2. Click inside that bar, type the letters **`http`** (all lowercase), and press **Enter**.
3. The background of the bar should turn green, and your screen will clean up drastically. You will now only see a few lines.

---

### Step 5: Answering Question (a) and (b)

1. Look at the top pane (the list of packets). Look down the **Info** column for a line that starts with **`GET /wireshark-labs/HTTP-wireshark-file1.html...`**.
2. **Click once** on that `GET` line to highlight it.
3. Now look at the **Middle Pane** of your screen. You will see a list of rows with little arrows/triangles next to them.
4. Look for the row that says **`Hypertext Transfer Protocol`** and click the little arrow next to it to expand it.

**Let's find your answers here:**

* **Your Browser's HTTP Version:** Look at the very first line right under *Hypertext Transfer Protocol*. It will say something like `GET /wireshark-labs/... HTTP/1.1`. That **HTTP/1.1** is your answer to part 1.
* **Accepted Languages (Question b):** Scroll down slightly inside that same expanded *Hypertext Transfer Protocol* section. Look for a line that says **`Accept-Language:`**. Whatever letters are next to it (like `en-US,en`) is your answer for the language!

---

### Step 6: Answering Questions (c), (d), and (e)

1. Go back up to the **Top Pane** list.
2. Right below the `GET` line you just clicked, you should see a line sent from the server that says **`HTTP/1.1 200 OK`** in the Info column. **Click on it.**
3. Look at the **Middle Pane** again. Ensure the **`Hypertext Transfer Protocol`** section is still expanded.

**Let's find the rest of your answers here:**

* **Status Code (Question c):** Look at the very first line under the protocol drop-down. It will say **`Status Code: 200`** or `HTTP/1.1 200 OK`. Your status code is **200 OK**.
* **Last Modified Time (Question d):** Scroll down a bit in those details. Look for a line that starts with **`Last-Modified:`**. It will show a date and time (e.g., *Fri, 22 May 2026...*). Write down exactly what your screen says.
* **Bytes Returned (Question e):** Look further down for a line that says **`Content-Length:`**. The number right next to it is the exact number of bytes returned to your browser.

Let's move on to the second URL! This one is designed specifically to test how your browser handles a **Conditional GET** and how caching works.

To get the exact results your teacher's question expects, we need to make sure we don't accidentally send a conditional request on the *first* attempt.

Follow these steps exactly to capture the clean data:

---

### Step 1: Prepare Your Capture

1. Open Wireshark and click the **Blue Shark Fin** button in the top left to start a brand new capture. (Click "Continue without Saving" if it asks).
2. Open a **New Incognito Window** (or Private Browsing tab) in your browser. This ensures no old copies of this second file are hidden in your browser's memory.
3. Copy and paste this second URL into the address bar, **but do not press Enter yet**:
**[http://gaia.cs.umass.edu/wireshark-labs/HTTP-wireshark-file2.html](http://gaia.cs.umass.edu/wireshark-labs/HTTP-wireshark-file2.html)**

---

### Step 2: The Two-Step Capture Process

**Action 1 (The First Request):**

1. Press **Enter** on your keyboard to load the website for the first time. You should see the text load on the screen.
2. Wait 5 seconds.

**Action 2 (The Second Request):**
3. Now, click the **Refresh/Reload** button on your browser (or press `F5`).
4. Go straight to Wireshark and click the **Red Square** button to stop the capture.
5. Type **`http`** into the top display filter bar and press Enter.

You should now see **two** `GET` lines and **two** response lines in your top pane list.

---

### Step 3: Finding Your Answers

#### **a. Inspect the contents of the first HTTP GET request. Do you see an “IF-MODIFIED-SINCE” line?**

* **How to find it:** Click on the **very first** `GET /wireshark-labs/HTTP-wireshark-file2.html` line at the top of your list. Look down the rows in the middle pane under `Hypertext Transfer Protocol`.
* **Your Answer:** **No.** There is no `If-Modified-Since` header present because the browser has never fetched this file before and has nothing to compare it against.

#### **b. Inspect the contents of the first server response. Did the server explicitly return the contents of the file? How can you tell?**

* **How to find it:** Click on the first server response line right below your first GET. Look at the **Info** column and the middle pane details.
* **Your Answer:** **Yes**, the server explicitly returned the contents of the file. We can tell because the status code is **200 OK**, and at the very bottom of the middle pane, you will see a dropdown called **"Line-based text data"** or **"File Data"** which contains the actual HTML text of the webpage.

#### **c. Inspect the contents of the second HTTP GET request. Do you see an “IF-MODIFIED-SINCE:” line? If so, what information follows it?**

* **How to find it:** Click on the **second** `GET` line in your top list (the one from when you refreshed). Expand `Hypertext Transfer Protocol` in the middle pane and scroll down.
* **Your Answer:** **Yes.** There is now an `If-Modified-Since` line. The information following it is the exact timestamp of when the file was last modified on the server (e.g., `If-Modified-Since: Wed, 20 May 2026 12:00:00 GMT`). Your browser copied this date from the first response it received.

#### **d. What is the HTTP status code and phrase returned from the server in response to this second HTTP GET? Did the server explicitly return the contents of the file? Explain.**

* **How to find it:** Click on the **second** server response line (the very last HTTP packet in your list). Look at the status line in the middle pane.
* **Your Answer:** The status code and phrase is **304 Not Modified**. No, the server **did not** explicitly return the contents of the file.
* **Explanation:** Because the file had not changed since the timestamp sent in the browser's `If-Modified-Since` header, the server sent a lightweight `304` response telling the browser it can save time and bandwidth by simply loading the webpage copy it already stored in its local cache.
